# Positioning, meaning and limits

*Draft positioning note; 2 November 2026. The proposed mathematical claims below remain subject to the proof audit. Approximately 900 words including references.*

The substantive question is how the organisation of learning affects the persistence of a cultural repertoire when each individual item has the same opportunity to spread. A population can learn through frequent encounters that transmit small fractions of a donor’s repertoire, or through fewer encounters that transmit larger fractions. Matching the effective transmission rate removes an elementary difference in learning opportunity and isolates dependence between traits. The resulting comparison concerns a defined inventory of socially acquired items; it does not equate inventory size with cultural sophistication, value or the totality of social life.

This question sits within an established anthropology of cultural linkage. Yeh, Fogarty and Kandler’s (2019) model allows trait links to form and break, producing packages that learners copy with imperfect success (§2b). Their simulations show that linked transmission can mimic signatures ordinarily attributed to transmission bias; §4c discusses the resulting limits of inference from population-level frequencies. That paper supplies substantive motivation, not the same mathematical problem: it concerns variants at fixed trait dimensions, permits package-dependent acquisition rates, and has no demographic replacement. The present comparison instead fixes marginal transmission while changing which traits share successful encounters. [Full primary text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6939269/fullTextXML)

The unresolved distributional question is explicit in Wakano and Aoki (2024, §§1.6.1–1.6.2). They distinguish shared potential role models with trait-specific copying, separately sampled models for each trait, and wholly shared cultural parents. These mechanisms can have the same expected ancestral counts, yet their genealogies have different dependence. They identify distributions beyond expectations as future work and the correlated case as particularly difficult. This provides a precise open-problem connection, although their discrete-generation model is not identical to the continuous-time model considered here. [Full primary text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11621620/)

Wakano et al. (2026) analyse whole-repertoire copying through a mixed-Poisson genealogy representation (equation 15). Their §4 then proposes independent copying probability (b<1) at a shared encounter, with encounter frequency increased by (1/b) to maintain effective transmission. Figure 16 explores this extension numerically. The candidate contribution addresses that particular extension analytically. It would establish an exact ordering across the entire parameter family, rather than extrapolate a trend from several simulated parameter choices. [Full primary text](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08677v2)

The originality claim must nevertheless be narrower than “means conceal correlations”. Baumdicker and Pfaffelhuber (2014, Remark 2.4) already describe joint gene-transfer/loss events with per-gene probabilities: holding the products γ′(p_\gamma) and ρ′(p_\rho) fixed leaves first moments unchanged, while second moments differ. Their §6.3 also supplies paired genealogies and overlap-state moment recurrences. Thus shared-event effects and the computational method have close prior art. The potential advance is a theorem specifying the **direction and full strength of the comparison**, plus an inverse consequence if strictness is proved. [Full primary text](https://arxiv.org/html/1301.6547v2)

For the specified neutral model, the proposed convex order says that larger (b), with compensating encounter frequency, increases expected convex losses of repertoire size while preserving its mean. It consequently orders variance, expected shortfall below each target count, and the probability of a completely empty sample. If strict variance monotonicity is established for nondegenerate parameters, and (N,u,\mu,\beta) are known, the population variance can identify (b). That is stronger than observing different variances in examples. It is also weaker than claiming that one noisy archaeological sample identifies a learning mechanism: structural identifiability assumes exact model moments and specified nuisance parameters, whereas estimation must confront sampling uncertainty, detection, heterogeneous traits and demographic history.

Several limits are integral to the interpretation. Lower copying probability is accompanied by more encounters, so the theorem cannot recommend inaccurate learning at fixed effort. Stationary absence is not the frequency, speed or duration of historical collapse. Convex order does not order every threshold-crossing probability. Neutral, nonrecurrent innovations, homogeneous transmission and complete repertoire clearing at death are modelling commitments rather than established descriptions of a particular population. An inference programme would therefore need a trait definition, an observation model and independently justified demographic constraints before translating the result into historical explanation.

The literature gate retains two documented access limits. Kobayashi et al. (2018) was inspected through its abstract and the authors’ detailed 2024 discussion; the three author publication sites supply no open manuscript. Scheie et al. (2026), published online on 23 October, reports a stationary spectrum and Gaussian asymptotics for repeated copying and forgetting. Its publisher metadata indicate open access, but retrieval failed; Jansson’s author page lists it without a manuscript link. Neither missing full text should be represented as fully checked. The current result is a promising mathematical contribution to a specific anthropological modelling question, with its eventual significance and priority still requiring scrutiny.
